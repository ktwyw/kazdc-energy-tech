How the hourly calculation works

The plant first applies the original dry-cooler controller. Each summer hour, the snow loop can take the smallest of the residual chiller load, the rated useful exchanger duty and the remaining usable energy divided by one hour. The backup chiller removes the balance. A chiller-off hour requires zero mechanical duty for the whole hourly balance. Dry-cooler dispatch equations

Storage. Initial latent energy in kWh is compacted volume × ice-equivalent density × 333.5 / 3,600. The usable fraction combines survival through the hold and accessible ice after reserve. On 1 June this sets the budget for useful heat delivery over the whole summer. Allow for expected storage losses and inaccessible ice when setting this fraction. The calculation assumes a constant useful exchanger duty while stock lasts. Site measurements could resolve heat leakage and changing water–ice contact hour by hour.

Electricity. The buffered loop cycles the snow pump at rated duty. Pump energy per kWh of delivered heat equals rated pump input divided by rated exchanger duty. Discharge requires this ratio to be below 1/COP. The annual saving compares the snow plant with the dry cooler plus chiller, then subtracts the entered collection electricity. The net can be negative.

Sizing. The target volume is the requested heat divided by usable energy per cubic metre. It can meet the target only if the eligible hours and useful exchanger duty allow that much heat transfer. The hot-hour policy uses each hour’s outdoor temperature and retains any unused reserve. Exchanger duty is an assumed net rating achievable at the selected supply temperature. The IT heat load stays fixed. Weather determines how much cooling the chiller must supply after dry cooling. Compacted volume and density are independent site inputs.

Warm return water can find channels through the snow. A smaller wetted ice surface limits the heat transfer rate, even while ice remains. During discharge, enclosure heat gain (228 W) and pump heat (40 W) melt a further 45.03 kWh of ice over seven days. For reference contact, the initial 1,198.88 kWh therefore covers 840.00 kWh of useful cooling and leaves 313.86 kWh of ice. The replay aggregates the audited case-A traces to one-hour intervals. Equipment and field measurements

Which measurements would show whether a store performs as the model predicts? Each component in the building information model (BIM) can be linked to a measurable quantity, from heat entering the store to electricity drawn by the chiller.

Store and insulation

Record fill volume, ice-equivalent density, footprint and cover construction. Weigh representative samples, survey the changing volume, and log cover and base heat flux to check the holding loss.

Sump and snow contact

Record the sump level, meltwater temperature and return-water distribution. A rising outlet temperature at the same load and flow can identify loss of contact. Measure drainage and conductivity to account for escaping water and dissolved salts.

Plate exchanger and clean loop

The meltwater circuit and clean coolant loop exchange heat across plates. Measure both flow rates and all four port temperatures. Compare each side’s mass flow × specific heat × temperature change with the assumed useful duty.

Pumps, chiller and handling

Submeter the snow pump and chiller package and log operating hours. Weigh collected snow and record electric and fuel inputs for additional hauling and compaction. Compare with a dry-cooler-plus-chiller run under the same load and weather.

Snow sources and scenario assumptions. The technology has field precedents in Sweden and Japan. Only the facility plant uses Astana’s hourly typical year. The three-store comparison uses 91 days of assumed heat gain for each store. The seven-day model replay uses a prescribed summer boundary following 120 days of storage.
